Cancer Awareness

Knowledge is the first form of care.

General information on cancer, early detection and the questions worth asking your health provider.

Why This Matters

Silence helps cancer. Information helps people.

A great deal of fear around cancer comes from not knowing — not knowing the warning signs, not knowing where to go, not knowing what a diagnosis really means. Part of our work is closing that gap in plain language, in our own community.

The information on this page is general health education. It is not a diagnosis and it is not a substitute for seeing a qualified health worker.

Important. If you notice a symptom that worries you, do not wait and do not self-treat. Visit a health facility and ask to be examined. Early questions are always cheaper than late answers.
SIGNS
Know The Signs

Changes worth getting checked.

None of these mean a person has cancer. They mean it is worth being examined rather than waiting.

A lump or swelling

Any new lump, thickening or swelling anywhere on the body — including the breast, neck, armpit, groin or testicles.

A sore that will not heal

Wounds, ulcers or mouth sores that stay open for weeks, or a mole that changes in size, shape or colour.

Unusual bleeding

Bleeding or discharge that is not normal for you — including between periods, after menopause, or in stool or urine.

A cough or hoarseness that persists

A cough, hoarse voice or difficulty swallowing that continues for several weeks without a clear cause.

Unexplained weight loss

Losing weight noticeably without trying, or long-lasting tiredness that rest does not fix.

A lasting change in habit

A persistent change in bowel or bladder habits, or ongoing indigestion that does not settle.

Myths & Facts

Replacing rumour with information.

These are the beliefs we meet most often in community conversations.

MYTH
Cancer is a curse, or a punishment for something a person did.
FACT
Cancer is a disease of the body's cells. It is not witchcraft, punishment or shame — and treating it as such delays people from seeking care.
MYTH
Cancer is contagious — you can catch it by sharing food, a bed or a home.
FACT
Cancer cannot be passed from person to person through touch, food, utensils or living together. People living with cancer need company, not isolation.
MYTH
A cancer diagnosis is an automatic death sentence, so there is no point going to hospital.
FACT
Outcomes depend heavily on the type of cancer and how early it is found. Many cancers respond well to treatment when detected early — which is exactly why late presentation is so costly.
MYTH
Herbal or spiritual treatment alone can cure cancer, so hospital treatment can be stopped.
FACT
Abandoning medical treatment allows cancer to advance. Faith and support matter deeply to many people — but they work alongside medical care, not instead of it.
MYTH
If nobody in my family has had cancer, I cannot get it.
FACT
Most people diagnosed with cancer have no family history of it. Family history raises risk for some cancers, but its absence is not protection.
Early Action

What to do if something feels wrong.

You do not need a diagnosis to be taken seriously. You only need a concern.

Write down what you have noticed

What changed, where, and roughly when it started. This makes the conversation with a clinician far more useful.

Go to a health facility and ask to be examined

Start with the nearest clinic or health centre. Ask directly whether screening or referral is appropriate for your symptom.

Ask questions until you understand the answer

What is being tested for, when results are expected, what the next step is, and what it will cost. Bring someone with you if that helps you remember.

Follow through on referral

If you are referred, go. A referral that is never followed is the most common place where early detection is lost.

Tell someone you trust

Facing it alone is the hardest way to face it. If you would like to talk to people who understand the journey, reach out to us.

Reducing Risk

Not everything is preventable. Some things are.

No habit guarantees protection. These are widely recognised ways to reduce risk.

Avoid tobacco

Tobacco in any form — smoked or chewed — is among the most clearly established causes of cancer.

Limit alcohol

Reducing alcohol intake lowers the risk of several cancers.

Vaccination

HPV and hepatitis B vaccination protect against infections linked to cervical and liver cancer. Ask your health provider what is available.

Screening

Cervical and breast screening can find changes before symptoms appear. Ask which screening applies to you and how often.

This page gives general health information only. For advice about your own health, screening schedule or treatment, speak with a qualified health professional.
