Cancer Resources

Why This Matters

Silence helps cancer. Information helps people.

A great deal of fear around cancer comes from not knowing: not knowing the warning signs, not knowing where to go, not knowing what a diagnosis really means. Part of our work is closing that gap in plain language, in our own community.

The information on this page is general health education. It is not a diagnosis and it is not a substitute for seeing a qualified health worker.

Important. If you notice a symptom that worries you, do not wait and do not self-treat. Visit a health facility and ask to be examined. Early questions are always cheaper than late answers.
TYPES
Common Cancers In Kenya

Five cancers make up most of the cases we see nationally.

According to the National Cancer Institute of Kenya, breast, cervical, prostate, oesophageal and colorectal cancers are consistently the most commonly diagnosed cancers in the country. Knowing the basics of each helps you recognize what is worth having checked.

Breast Cancer

The most commonly diagnosed cancer among Kenyan women. A new lump, change in breast shape or size, skin dimpling, or nipple discharge are reasons to get examined promptly.

Self-exam monthly from age 20; clinical exam every 1–3 years; mammogram from 40 where available

Cervical Cancer

Almost always linked to long-term infection with certain strains of HPV. It develops slowly, which makes it one of the most preventable cancers when screening happens regularly.

VIA or Pap smear every 3 years from age 25; HPV vaccination for girls before sexual debut

Prostate Cancer

The most common cancer among Kenyan men, usually appearing after age 50. Difficulty urinating, a weak stream, or blood in urine or semen are worth a clinic visit.

PSA blood test and clinical discussion from age 50 (from 45 with a family history)

Oesophageal Cancer

Affects the food pipe and is linked to tobacco, alcohol and very hot beverages taken over many years. Difficulty or pain swallowing, and unintended weight loss, are the earliest clues.

No routine population screening; reporting symptoms early matters most

Colorectal Cancer

Cancer of the colon or rectum, linked to diet, inactivity and age. Blood in stool, a lasting change in bowel habits, or persistent abdominal pain deserve medical attention.

Stool test or colonoscopy from age 45–50, or earlier with a family history

Beyond These Five

Skin, liver, lung, thyroid, blood and childhood cancers also affect people in our community. If a symptom worries you, it is always worth being examined, whatever the type.

Screening ages and intervals vary by individual and family risk and by national guidance, which is periodically updated. Always confirm your personal schedule with a qualified health provider.

SIGNS
Know The Signs

Changes worth getting checked.

None of these mean a person has cancer. They mean it is worth being examined rather than waiting.

A lump or swelling

Any new lump, thickening or swelling anywhere on the body, including the breast, neck, armpit, groin or testicles.

A sore that will not heal

Wounds, ulcers or mouth sores that stay open for weeks, or a mole that changes in size, shape or colour.

Unusual bleeding

Bleeding or discharge that is not normal for you, including between periods, after menopause, or in stool or urine.

A cough or hoarseness that persists

A cough, hoarse voice or difficulty swallowing that continues for several weeks without a clear cause.

Unexplained weight loss

Losing weight noticeably without trying, or long-lasting tiredness that rest does not fix.

A lasting change in habit

A persistent change in bowel or bladder habits, or ongoing indigestion that does not settle.

Myths & Facts

Replacing rumour with information.

These are the beliefs we meet most often in community conversations.

Common cancer myths in Trans Nzoia County and the corresponding facts
Myth Fact
Cancer is a curse, or a punishment for something a person did. Cancer is a disease of the body's cells. It is not witchcraft, punishment or shame, and treating it as such delays people from seeking care.
Cancer is contagious. You can catch it by sharing food, a bed or a home. Cancer cannot be passed from person to person through touch, food, utensils or living together. People living with cancer need company, not isolation.
A cancer diagnosis is an automatic death sentence, so there is no point going to hospital. Outcomes depend heavily on the type of cancer and how early it is found. Many cancers respond well to treatment when detected early, which is exactly why late presentation is so costly.
Herbal or spiritual treatment alone can cure cancer, so hospital treatment can be stopped. Abandoning medical treatment allows cancer to advance. Faith and support matter deeply to many people, but they work alongside medical care, not instead of it.
If nobody in my family has had cancer, I cannot get it. Most people diagnosed with cancer have no family history of it. Family history raises risk for some cancers, but its absence is not protection.

Early Action

What to do if something feels wrong.

You do not need a diagnosis to be taken seriously. You only need a concern.

Write down what you have noticed

What changed, where, and roughly when it started. This makes the conversation with a clinician far more useful.

Go to a health facility and ask to be examined

Start with the nearest clinic or health centre. Ask directly whether screening or referral is appropriate for your symptom.

Ask questions until you understand the answer

What is being tested for, when results are expected, what the next step is, and what it will cost. Bring someone with you if that helps you remember.

Follow through on referral

If you are referred, go. A referral that is never followed is the most common place where early detection is lost.

Tell someone you trust

Facing it alone is the hardest way to face it. If you would like to talk to people who understand the journey, reach out to us.

Reducing Risk

Not everything is preventable. Some things are.

No habit guarantees protection. These are widely recognised ways to reduce risk.

Avoid tobacco

Tobacco in any form, smoked or chewed, is among the most clearly established causes of cancer.

Limit alcohol

Reducing alcohol intake lowers the risk of several cancers.

Vaccination

HPV and hepatitis B vaccination protect against infections linked to cervical and liver cancer. Ask your health provider what is available.

Screening

Cervical and breast screening can find changes before symptoms appear. Ask which screening applies to you and how often.

This page gives general health information only. For advice about your own health, screening schedule or treatment, speak with a qualified health professional.
Plain-Language Glossary

Words you might hear at the hospital.

A cancer diagnosis comes with a lot of new vocabulary, often delivered quickly and in a stressful moment. Here is what the most common terms actually mean.

A small sample of tissue removed from the body and examined under a microscope to confirm whether cancer is present and, if so, what type it is. It is usually the only way to be certain of a diagnosis.

A doctor who specializes in diagnosing and treating cancer. You may meet more than one: a medical oncologist (medicine), a surgical oncologist (operations), and a radiation oncologist (radiotherapy).

A malignant growth is cancerous and can spread to other parts of the body. A benign growth is not cancerous and generally does not spread, though it can still sometimes need treatment.

Medicine that travels through the bloodstream to kill fast-growing cells, including cancer cells. It is usually given in cycles, with rest periods in between to let the body recover.

Treatment that uses targeted high-energy rays to destroy cancer cells in a specific area of the body, while trying to limit harm to the surrounding healthy tissue.

The spread of cancer cells from where they started to another part of the body. A cancer that has metastasized is often described by its stage, which affects treatment options.

A period where signs and symptoms of cancer are reduced or have disappeared. Remission can be partial or complete, and does not always mean the cancer is permanently gone, so ongoing check-ups still matter.

Specialized care focused on relieving pain and other symptoms and improving quality of life for a person with a serious illness. It can happen alongside curative treatment, not only at the end of life.
